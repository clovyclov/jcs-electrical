# Avoid AI Writing

Copy for this site should read like it came from a working electrical contractor, not a
language model. This skill is a self-review checklist: draft the copy, then run it against
the sections below before calling it done. Most of these patterns aren't wrong in isolation —
the problem is they show up constantly in AI output and rarely in how a small business actually
talks to its customers, so a reader (or a search engine's spam filters) clocks them fast.

## 1. Banned vocabulary

These words are statistically over-represented in AI-generated marketing copy. If one shows
up in a draft, replace it with something plainer — usually a shorter, more concrete word does
the same job.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| boasts, showcases | has, offers, comes with |
| stands as a testament to | shows, proves |
| underscores, highlights the importance of | shows, matters because |
| delve into | look at, cover |
| navigate (the process/landscape) | handle, walk you through |
| landscape (e.g. "electrical landscape") | drop it entirely, or say "electrical work" |
| tapestry | drop it entirely |
| elevate | improve, upgrade |
| foster | build, support |
| leverage (as a verb) | use |
| pivotal, crucial, vital | important, or just cut it |
| myriad, plethora, a wide array of | many, several, or list them |
| seamless, seamlessly | smooth, or just describe what happens |
| robust | solid, reliable, built to last |
| vibrant, dynamic | cut it — usually adds nothing |
| unwavering commitment to | committed to, or state the actual commitment |
| game-changer, game-changing | cut it |
| at the heart of | central to, or restructure the sentence |
| in today's world, in the ever-evolving landscape of | cut the throat-clearing, start with the point |
| ensure peace of mind | say what actually gives the customer confidence (license #, warranty, response time) |

If a sentence needs two or three of these to sound "professional," the sentence is doing too
much work with too little content — cut it down to a specific claim instead.

## 2. Stock transitions and hedges

AI copy leans on formal essay connectors that a tradesperson's website never uses. Cut these
outright rather than replacing them — the sentences almost always work fine without them:

- "It's important to note that..." / "It's worth noting that..."
- "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," (as sentence openers)
- "In conclusion," "In summary," "Overall," (as a way to end a section)
- "No discussion of X would be complete without..."
- "When it comes to..."
- Knowledge/capability disclaimers that never belong on a business site anyway ("I don't have
  real-time information," "as of my last update") — these should never appear, but check for
  them if copy was drafted by an AI tool and pasted in.

## 3. Puffery vs. real claims

A local business page is allowed to be warm and a little proud of its work — that's not the
problem. The problem is generic superlatives that could describe literally any electrician
and that nobody can verify:

- Avoid: "breathtaking results," "stunning transformation," "rich history of excellence,"
  "unparalleled service," "commitment to excellence."
- Instead: name the specific, checkable thing — years in business, license number, a specific
  warranty length, response time, a specific neighborhood served, a real number (jobs completed,
  years of experience). Specificity reads as trustworthy; adjective stacking reads as filler.

Rule of thumb: if you deleted the sentence, would the page lose real information? If not, cut it
rather than trying to fix the wording.

## 4. Structural tics

- **Rule-of-three overload.** AI writing constantly reaches for three parallel items
  ("fast, reliable, and affordable") to make a claim sound complete. One well-chosen detail
  beats a rehearsed triplet — don't pad a list to exactly three just because it sounds balanced.
- **"Not just X, but Y" / negative parallelism.** ("We're not just electricians, we're your
  partners in home safety.") This construction is everywhere in AI copy and almost nowhere in
  how people actually talk. State the point directly instead.
- **Bolded bullet-list dumps.** A list where every bullet starts with a **Bolded Term:**
  followed by a sentence is a very recognizable chatbot pattern. Use bullets when they hold a
  genuine list (services offered, steps in a process); don't convert plain paragraphs into bullets
  just to look scannable.
- **Title-Case Section Headers** used inconsistently, or headers on every single subsection
  including ones with one sentence under them. Keep headers sentence case and only where they
  earn their keep.
- **Em dash overuse.** AI output reaches for em dashes constantly, often where a comma, period,
  or colon would read more naturally. If a paragraph has more than one em dash, check whether
  a period or comma works better.
- **Section-ending recaps.** Don't close a page section by restating what it just said
  ("In short, our licensed electricians deliver..."). End on the last real point or the CTA.

## 5. Quick before/after

- Before: "Our team boasts decades of combined experience, ensuring seamless electrical
  solutions that elevate your home's safety and reliability."
- After: "We've been wiring homes in [area] for over 20 years. Licensed, insured, and up
  front about pricing before we start."

- Before: "When it comes to panel upgrades, it's important to note that outdated panels can
  pose serious risks. Our team doesn't just fix the problem — we provide peace of mind."
- After: "An outdated panel is a fire risk. We'll inspect yours, tell you straight whether it
  needs replacing, and give you a flat quote before any work starts."

## Self-review checklist

Before treating a piece of copy as final, scan it for:

- [ ] Any word from the banned-vocabulary table above
- [ ] Any stock transition or hedge from section 2
- [ ] Generic superlatives with no specific, checkable claim behind them
- [ ] A list padded to exactly three items, or a "not just X, but Y" construction
- [ ] Bolded-bullet-dump formatting where plain prose or a real list would read better
- [ ] More than one em dash in a paragraph
- [ ] A section that ends by summarizing itself instead of ending on the point

If a draft trips several of these at once, don't patch individual words — rewrite the passage
starting from the one or two specific, concrete things it's actually trying to say.
